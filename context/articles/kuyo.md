## THE SUBJECT

**供養 (kuyō)** is a Buddhist memorial service. In Japan it is held not only for the dead but for
**objects that have finished their service** — needles, brushes, dolls, combs, spectacles, tools,
and in modern form the machinery a company has run for decades. Tom saw a piece Rieko shared
showing workers paying respects to machines they had used for years, and was struck by it.

**Cover kuyō as a whole, then go into the specifics** (Tom's decision, 8 Sep 2026). The specifics:

- **針供養 (hari-kuyō)**, the needle memorial, is the best-known. Roughly 400 years old. Held
  **8 February in western Japan and 8 December in the east.** Seamstresses, tailors, embroiderers
  and weavers bring broken and bent needles to a shrine and push them into a block of **tofu or
  konnyaku** — something soft, so the needles rest after a working life spent piercing hard cloth.
  That detail is the heart of the whole idea and should carry the piece.
- **道具供養 (dōgu-kuyō)** for tools, **筆供養 (fude-kuyō)** for brushes, **人形供養
  (ningyō-kuyō)** for dolls.
- Underneath sits **mottainai** (not wasting, honouring small things) and the older belief that a
  life force resides in all things — which is Auwa's whole foundation.
- Adjacent and worth a line, not a detour: **付喪神 (tsukumogami)**, the folk idea that an object
  which survives long enough acquires a spirit of its own.

**Verify every one of these before publishing.** They were researched, not remembered.

Yaoyorozu no Kami with a receipt.

## THE HONEST PROBLEM: THERE ARE NO KUYŌ PHOTOGRAPHS

Tom and Rieko have not photographed a kuyō. The archive holds no ceremony, no needles in tofu, no
shrine on 8 February. This is the piece with the best idea and the weakest first-hand material, and
that tension has to be resolved deliberately rather than papered over. Three options:

1. **Write it now, illustrated with objects that carry use.** The photographs below are craftsmen's
   hands, worn tools, aged timber and objects made to last. They support the *idea* honestly
   without pretending to document the ritual. Captions must not imply otherwise.
2. **Hold it and photograph a real hari-kuyō** on a future Japan trip — 8 February (west, and
   Rieko is from Kansai) or 8 December. That would make it the definitive English-language piece
   on the subject, with photographs nobody else has. It is also a year away.
3. **Write it from Rieko's own experience.** She grew up inside this. If she has a memory of a
   kuyō, or of being taught to thank an object, that is first-hand and the article stands on it.
   **Ask her first — this may dissolve the whole problem.**

**Recommendation: ask Rieko before deciding.** If she has the memory, take option 3 now and
consider photographing a real one later as a follow-up. If she does not, option 2 is worth the
wait, because this subject deserves to be done properly and the competition is thin enough that
arriving late costs little.

## ANGLE AND SEO

Genuinely winnable. Competition on hari-kuyō is thin: a Wikipedia stub, a few craft blogs, an
oddity-news piece. No one has written it as considered editorial, and **Beth Kempton has not taken
this term** (see the philosophy-cluster note in `context/pillar/photography.md` — she owns wabi
sabi and kokoro, which is why the cluster should be built from the specific terms outward).

- **Primary keyword:** hari-kuyo / kuyo (Japanese memorial service for objects)
- **Secondary:** mottainai, tsukumogami, needle memorial, dogu-kuyo
- **`title`** (poetic H1): OPEN. Something about thanking a needle.
- **`seoTitle`** (max 45 chars): must contain the searched term, e.g. *Kuyō: Japan's Memorial for Objects*
- **One clean quotable definition near the top.** Concept articles that carry a crisp definition
  win featured snippets and get cited in AI answers — this is how the Narai article ended up in
  Google's AI Overview.
- Internal links: `yaoyorozu-no-kami` (essential, both ways), `oroku-gushi` and `shigefusa-knife`
  (objects made to last), later `kokoro`.
